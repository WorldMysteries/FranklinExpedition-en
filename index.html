Polar Expedition · 1845–1848

TheFRANKLIN EXPEDITION

Two ships and 129 men set out to find the Northwest Passage — the last uncharted stretch on the map of the world. The best-equipped expedition of its age: steam engines, central heating, three years of food.

None returned. The wrecks of Erebus and Terror were found only in 2014 and 2016 — and we still do not know what happened to the men between those dates.

    What was done before departure

    Refit · spring 1845
    01⚓

    Ice armour

    The bow was sheathed in iron and extra oak, with diagonal bracing and cross-beams inside: the hull was built to absorb the recoil of mortars.

    02⚙

    Steam and screw

    Twenty-five horsepower each, from railway locomotives; the screw and rudder could be hoisted into wells so the ice could not tear them off. Under steam: about four knots.

    03♨

    Heat

    The Sylvester system: hot air ducted through the decks and hot-water pipes along the living quarters. A first for the polar fleet.

    04≈

    Fresh water

    Desalinators fed from the ship's boilers, tanks holding thousands of litres — plus lemon juice against scurvy.

    05▤

    Three years of provisions

    About 62 tons of flour, 16.7 tons of salt beef, 8,000 Goldner's tinned meats, tea, sugar, chocolate, dried vegetables.

    06✧

    Science and daily life

    Magnetic instruments, botanical presses, schoolbooks for the men, a barrel organ, masks and costumes for a masquerade ball.

    19 May 1845

    Departure from Greenhithe

    134men aboard

    Two bomb vessels leave the Thames: the finest polar ships Britain had, three years of provisions, steam engines taken from railway locomotives, 1,200 books aboard Erebus. The expedition is seen off as a triumph: only the last links of the Northwest Passage remained to be closed.

    July 1845

    Greenland: the last letters

    129remained with the expedition

    In Disko Bay five members of the crew are sent home aboard transport ships. The rest write to their families — these letters will be the last words from the expedition to reach home. The officers send their love and promise to return through the Pacific.

    late July 1845

    The last witnesses

    The whalers Dannett and Martin meet the ships in Baffin Bay. Franklin is waiting for a lead to cross the bay toward Lancaster Sound. Morale is excellent. After this — silence for nine years.

    winter 1845–1846

    Beechey Island: three graves

    3first deaths

    The first winter goes well: the ships lie in a harbour, the workshops are busy, magnetic surveys are under way. But in January they bury stoker John Torrington and seaman John Hartnell; in April, marine William Braine.

    12 September 1846

    The ice closes

    The ships are beset in Victoria Strait northwest of King William Island. The summer of 1846 was cold, and multi-year ice never left the shore. They will not break free in 1847, nor in 1848.

    24 May 1847

    “All well”

    Lieutenant Graham Gore and Mate Des Voeux leave a standard Admiralty form in a cairn at Victory Point with a confident note: wintered, ascended to 77° N, a party of two officers and six men gone ashore. Three weeks later Franklin will be dead.

    11 June 1847

    The death of Sir John Franklin

    24dead by spring 1848

    The commander dies aboard Erebus at the age of 60. The cause is unknown. Command passes to Francis Crozier — an Irishman, veteran of four polar winters, the most experienced ice captain in Britain.

    22 April 1848

    The ships are abandoned

    105set out on foot

    After 19 months in the ice, 105 survivors come ashore at Victory Point. They drag boats loaded with supplies and possessions south toward Back River. Nine officers and fifteen men are already dead. The march begins from which no one will return.

    25 April 1848

    The note in the margin

    Crozier and Fitzjames write along the edges of the same form — in handwriting cramped by the margins: the date the ships were abandoned, the number of men, Franklin's death, the course for Back River. The document stays in the cairn for eleven years.

    1848–1859

    The great searches

    30+search expeditions

    Lady Jane Franklin refuses to accept that her husband is dead and campaigns for new expeditions. The Admiralty and private donors send dozens of ships. The searches do not find the men, but they chart almost the whole of Arctic Canada.

    April 1854

    John Rae and the terrible news

    The Scottish surgeon and traveller brings to London Inuit accounts of bodies, a boat, and traces of cannibalism, along with silver spoons bearing officers' crests. Society meets it with fury: Dickens publicly calls the evidence “the vague babble of savages.”

    May 1859

    McClintock finds the note

    The Fox expedition, paid for by Lady Franklin, finally obtains proof: the Victory Point note, a boat with two skeletons at Erebus Bay, dozens of relics and Inuit testimonies. Officially: the expedition perished, but it had discovered the Northwest Passage.

    1869–1880

    Hall and Schwatka among the Inuit

    The Americans Charles Francis Hall and Frederick Schwatka live for years among the Netsilik Inuit and record oral testimony. Their maps will match the places of future discoveries — with an accuracy that Victorian search parties never achieved.

    1981–1986

    The exhumations at Beechey

    The anthropologist Owen Beattie raises the bodies of the three sailors. Their hair and bones show high levels of lead; Torrington's lungs show tuberculosis and pneumonia. The argument over lead poisoning has not ended.

    2 September 2014

    Erebus is found

    169 years after the departure, a Canadian team finds the ship in only 11 metres of water — south of every area the Victorian searchers combed. The ship had been shifted by ice. Inuit oral history pointed the way.

    3 September 2016

    Terror is found

    In Terror Bay, northwest of King William Island, the second ship is discovered — almost perfectly preserved, with glass in the windows and dishes on the shelves. Inuit had spoken of the place for decades.

    2021 · 2024 · 2026

    DNA gives names back

    6identified by DNA

    Geneticists from the University of Waterloo and Lakehead University identify engineer John Gregory (2021), Captain James Fitzjames (2024, with cut marks on his jaw) and four more sailors (2026), including able seaman Harry Peglar. In all, six of the 129 have been named by DNA.

    today

    The excavation continues

    Parks Canada's underwater archaeologists work through holes cut in an ice dome, raising pistols, coins, thermometers, drawings and shoes. The artefacts belong jointly to Canada and the Inuit Heritage Trust and are displayed in Gjoa Haven. The full picture of the tragedy still eludes us.

    03Map

    Twenty miles
    from open water

    The ships were trapped in Victoria Strait — where multi-year ice bears down from the north. Both wrecks were found far south and north of the nineteenth-century search areas: the ice carried them away after the men left.

      ✧  Note 4

      For eleven years this sheet lay among the stones, three hundred metres from where the ships were being sought. Everything we know about the last days begins with it.

      05People

      129 men.
      Not one survivor.

      Officers, seamen, Royal Marines, surgeons, engineers, caulkers and boys. Three are buried on Beechey Island, twenty-one died aboard the ships, one hundred and five set out on foot — and all perished.

      06Searches

      They looked for men —
      and charted the Arctic

      More than thirty expeditions in twelve years, dozens of ships, millions of pounds. Not one man saved. The by-product of the search was almost the whole map of the Canadian North — and a Northwest Passage finally traversed from the west.

      ✦  The principal search parties
      1848–1849

      James Clark Ross

      Enterprise, Investigator

      The first wave, searching from the east. They found only ice; wintered off Somerset Island.

      1848–1851

      Henry Kellett

      Herald

      Approached from the west through Bering Strait; discovered Herald Island. No men.

      1850–1851

      William Penny, Erasmus Ommanney

      Admiralty squadron

      Three graves, traces of a wintering and mountains of empty tins were found on Beechey Island.

      1850–1854

      Robert McClure

      Investigator

      Traversed the Northwest Passage from the west, but abandoned his ship in the ice of Mercy Bay.

      1852–1854

      Edward Belcher

      Five ships

      The largest and most expensive operation. Four vessels abandoned in the ice; Franklin not found.

      1853–1854

      John Rae

      On foot, with boats

      Collected Inuit testimony and officers' silver. Brought back the first truthful answer — and was rejected.

      1857–1859

      Francis McClintock

      Fox

      Paid for by Lady Franklin. Found the Victory Point note, the boat with skeletons, and dozens of relics.

      1878–1880

      Frederick Schwatka

      Sledges, two years in the tundra

      The first expedition built entirely on Inuit knowledge and methods of survival.

      The engine of the search

      Lady Jane Franklin

      She refused to accept that her husband was dead and for twelve years campaigned for new expeditions: writing to the Admiralty, to presidents and to monarchs, spending her own fortune. When the state stopped, she fitted out the Fox herself — and that expedition found the Victory Point note.

      1847 — 1859 · five ships equipped

      ❝

      “The vague babble of savages” — Charles Dickens's phrase for the Inuit testimony about the fate of the expedition.

      In 1854 London chose to insult the witnesses rather than accept their evidence. A century and a half later, that evidence led to the wrecks of both ships.

      Inuit oral history

      Testimony that proved accurate
      Recorded by John Rae, 1854

      Inuit told of a party of white men dragging a boat upriver, of bodies in a tent and on the shore, of kettles, and of how it ended in starvation. Rae reported it verbatim — and was publicly accused of lying.

      Recorded by Charles Hall, 1869

      Accounts of a ship that lasted a long time and was crushed by ice after the men had left; of white men who fell and died along the road south, and of people who wintered nearby.

      Louie Kamookak, 1980s–2010s

      The Inuit historian from Gjoa Haven gathered dozens of oral accounts into a single map. His work, with the testimony of Sammy Kogvik, took the search vessels to Erebus and Terror.

      07The last march

      They dragged their boats
      toward life

      On 26 April 1848 one hundred and five men moved south along the west shore of King William Island. They hauled ship's boats on sledges, carrying books, soap, chocolate, silverware and toothbrushes. Inuit later found a boat turned bow-first back the other way: someone had tried to return to the ships.

      Six theories — and no complete answer

      Open a row
      For

      Raised lead was found in the hair and bones of Torrington, Hartnell and Braine. The tins at Beechey were indeed crudely and carelessly soldered.

      Against

      Later isotopic studies point to lead from the ships' systems and the water pipes of their home towns rather than the tins; the levels vary.

      For

      Signs of scurvy on bones from Erebus Bay; Inuit accounts of men with “black faces”; a boat packed with useless things instead of food.

      Against

      Scurvy is a consequence, not a first cause: it explains deaths on the march, but not why twenty-four men died aboard well-stocked ships.

      For

      Torrington's autopsy showed evidence of tuberculosis and pneumonia; he died in the very first winter.

      Against

      The three Beechey dead are not necessarily representative of the whole crew; the scale of disease is unknown.

      For

      Cut marks on roughly a quarter of the studied bones; in 2024 this identified Captain Fitzjames. Inuit had spoken of it as early as 1854.

      Against

      It is evidence of extreme starvation, not of “savagery”: it says nothing about the order and discipline of the preceding three years.

      For

      Dendrochronology and ice reconstructions confirm unusually severe seasons; the note records nineteen months of immobility.

      Against

      Why they did not leave earlier, and why they went south rather than east toward Simpson Strait, is not fully explained.

      For

      Inuit accounts of a ship that sank later; closed hatches, intact glass, objects in their places.

      Against

      Ice drift could also have moved the hull. There is no direct proof of reoccupation yet — the excavation continues.

      The full picture
      is unknown

      Every season of excavation adds objects — and new questions. The ships' logs, if they survive in the closed cabins, could tell everything. They have not yet been found.

      In memory

      129 names
      carved in Greenwich

      The memorial in the courtyard of the Old Royal Naval College lists them all — from rear admiral to ship's boy. For a long time it was believed that they died as heroes struggling with the ice. The truth proved more complicated and more terrible, and it is still being assembled from bones, tin cans and Inuit testimony.
